# Procedures

Lay first aid and basic procedures, written for drawing. Each entry gives who it applies to, the steps in order with the landmark (names from `anatomy.md`) and the number that matters, what to do if it fails, and the mistakes worth a "do not".

These follow the consensus of ILCOR-based guidelines (European Resuscitation Council, American Heart Association, Red Cross) as commonly taught. Guidelines are revised about every five years and differ in details between bodies and countries, so when a search tool is available, confirm the entry against the current guideline for the reader's country and put that source and year on the sheet. Points known to differ are marked **Varies**.

The emergency number differs by country (112 in the EU, 911 in North America and Mexico, 999 in the UK, 000 in Australia). Use the reader's if known; otherwise write "the emergency number".

## 1. Unresponsive person: check and call
1. Check the scene is safe for you.
2. Tap the shoulders and shout. No response: shout for help.
3. Open the airway: one hand on the `forehead`, two fingers under the `chin`, tilt the head back.
4. Look, listen and feel for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. Occasional gasps are not breathing.
5. Not breathing normally: call the emergency number (speaker phone), send someone for an AED, start CPR (2). Breathing normally: recovery position (5) and call.

Do not: leave them on their back if breathing but unresponsive; spend more than 10 seconds checking.

## 2. CPR, adult
1. Person on their back on a firm surface. Kneel beside the chest.
2. Heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, on the lower half of the breastbone (`sternum`); other hand on top, fingers interlocked.
3. Arms straight, shoulders directly above the hands. Push straight down 5–6 cm (about 2 in), then release fully. Rate 100–120 per minute.
4. After 30 compressions give 2 rescue breaths: tilt the head, pinch the nose, seal the mouth, breathe for 1 second each, watch the chest rise. Ratio **30 : 2**.
5. Unwilling or untrained in breaths: compressions only, without stopping.
6. Continue until an AED is ready, help takes over, or the person breathes normally. Swap rescuers about every 2 minutes.

Draw: side view, casualty supine, rescuer kneeling beyond the chest, arms vertical and straight, a `both: true` arrow beside the arms, count "30 : 2" or "100–120/min".

Do not: bend the elbows; press on the ribs, the tip of the breastbone (`xiphoid`) or the abdomen; pause compressions for more than 10 seconds.

## 3. CPR, child and infant
- **Child (1 year to puberty):** one or two hands on the lower half of the breastbone; depth about one third of the chest (about 5 cm).
- **Infant (under 1 year):** depth one third of the chest (about 4 cm); head in a neutral position, not tilted far back; breaths cover the infant's mouth and nose. **Varies:** two fingers just below the nipple line is the older teaching; the two-thumb encircling technique is now preferred for all rescuers in recent AHA and ERC guidance.
- Ratio: lay rescuers use 30 : 2. **Varies:** ERC paediatric guidance starts with 5 rescue breaths and trained rescuers use 15 : 2.
- A lone rescuer with a child who collapsed unwitnessed gives about 1 minute (or 5 cycles) of CPR before leaving to call, if there is no phone to hand.

Do not: over-tilt an infant's head; use adult force.

## 4. AED
1. Switch it on and follow its voice.
2. Bare the chest; dry it if wet.
3. Pads as pictured on them: one below the right collarbone, one on the left side below the armpit. (Front view; left and right are the casualty's.)
4. Nobody touching while it analyses. If it advises a shock: check all clear, press the button.
5. Resume compressions immediately; the AED re-analyses every 2 minutes.

Children under 8 years or 25 kg: paediatric pads or mode if available; if pads would touch, one on the centre of the chest and one on the back. Otherwise use adult pads.

Do not: delay CPR to look for an AED alone; touch the person during the shock; place pads over a medication patch or directly on an implanted device bulge.

## 5. Recovery position
For someone unresponsive but breathing normally, without suspected spinal injury.
1. Kneel beside them. Place the nearer arm out at a right angle, palm up.
2. Bring the far arm across the chest; hold the back of that hand against the nearer cheek.
3. Pull the far knee up so the foot is flat, then roll them toward you by pulling on that knee.
4. Tilt the head back to keep the airway open; the bent upper leg at right angles stops them rolling.
5. Call the emergency number. Check breathing continuously; if it stops, roll onto the back and start CPR.

Draw: the final position is the key panel. Side view: body on its side facing the viewer, lower arm forward, upper knee bent forward, head tilted back, mouth toward the floor.

Late pregnancy: lie on the left side.

## 6. Choking, adult and child over 1 year
Severe obstruction: cannot speak, cough or breathe; often hands at the `throat`.
1. Ask "Are you choking?" If they can cough, encourage coughing only.
2. Up to 5 back blows: stand to the side and a little behind, support the chest with one hand, lean them well forward, strike between the `shoulderBlades` with the heel of the hand. Check after each.
3. Up to 5 abdominal thrusts: stand behind, arms around, fist just above the `navel` (on `upperAbdomen`, well below the `xiphoid`), other hand over the fist, pull sharply inward and upward.
4. Alternate 5 and 5. Have someone call the emergency number.
5. Unresponsive: lower to the floor, call, start CPR; look in the mouth before breaths and remove only what is visible.

**Varies:** ERC and Red Cross teach back blows first. The AHA taught abdominal thrusts alone for many years; its 2025 guidelines also begin with back blows. Verify for the reader's country.

Pregnant or too large to reach around: chest thrusts (hands on the lower half of the breastbone) in place of abdominal thrusts. Small child: kneel behind.

Do not: blind finger sweeps; back blows on someone coughing effectively. Anyone given abdominal thrusts should be medically checked.

## 7. Choking, infant under 1 year
1. Sit or kneel. Lay the infant face down along your forearm, head lower than the chest, jaw supported in your hand (not the throat).
2. Up to 5 back blows between the `shoulderBlades` with the heel of the hand.
3. Turn face up along the other forearm, head low. Up to 5 chest thrusts on the lower half of the breastbone, just below the nipple line, sharp and slower than CPR compressions. **Varies:** two fingers is the long-standing teaching; recent AHA guidance is reported to prefer the heel of one hand. Verify and name the source.
4. Alternate 5 and 5; call the emergency number.
5. Unresponsive: infant CPR.

Do not: abdominal thrusts on an infant; hold upside down by the feet; blind finger sweeps.

Draw: `build: "infant"` lying along an adult forearm resting on the thigh of a seated rescuer; head clearly lower than the hips.

## 8. Severe bleeding and tourniquet
1. Call the emergency number. Gloves or a barrier if available.
2. Press firmly and directly on the wound with a clean cloth or dressing, with both hands if needed.
3. Keep pressing without lifting to look. If blood soaks through, add more on top; do not remove the first layer.
4. Bandage firmly over the dressing once it slows. Lay them down and keep them warm (shock).
5. Life-threatening limb bleeding that pressure does not control: tourniquet 5–7 cm (2–3 in) above the wound, not over a joint; tighten until bleeding stops; note the time; do not loosen.

Embedded object: do not remove it; press on either side and pad around it.

Do not: lift the dressing to check; use a tourniquet on the neck or torso; remove a tourniquet once on. Elevation and pressure points are no longer core steps in most guidance.

## 9. Burns and scalds
1. Stop the burning: away from the source, smother flames (stop, drop, roll), switch off power before touching an electrical casualty.
2. Cool under cool or lukewarm running water for **20 minutes**, as soon as possible (still worth doing up to 3 hours later). **Varies:** some bodies say at least 10 minutes.
3. While cooling, remove rings, watches and clothing near the burn, unless stuck to it.
4. Cover loosely with cling film laid lengthways (not wrapped around) or a clean non-fluffy dressing; a clean plastic bag for a hand.
5. Keep the person warm; cool the burn, not the patient.

Call the emergency number or go to hospital for: burns larger than the person's own hand, deep burns (white, charred or leathery; may be painless), burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals or over a joint, all chemical and electrical burns, any burn with smoke inhalation, and burns in children, older people or pregnancy. Chemical burns: brush off dry powder, then flush with water for at least 20 minutes.

Do not: ice or iced water; butter, oil, toothpaste or creams; burst blisters; peel off stuck clothing; fluffy dressings.

Draw: a forearm close-up under a tap with `drops` and "20 min" is the key panel. The do-not panel with `forbid` over an ice cube and a tube earns its place; these mistakes are common.

## 10. Nosebleed
1. Sit down, lean **forward**.
2. Pinch the soft part of the nose, just below the bony bridge, firmly.
3. Hold for 10–15 minutes without releasing; breathe through the mouth; spit out blood.
4. Afterwards do not blow or pick the nose for several hours.

Seek care if it lasts beyond 20–30 minutes, follows a head injury, or the person takes blood thinners.

Do not: tilt the head back (blood runs down the throat); pinch the bony bridge; pack the nose with tissue.

## 11. Sprains, strains and suspected fractures
Sprain or strain: rest, ice wrapped in cloth for up to 20 minutes at a time, comfortable compression bandage, elevate. (**Varies:** RICE, PRICE or newer "protect and load gradually" advice.)

Suspected fracture (deformity, cannot bear weight or use it, severe pain, grating):
1. Keep the limb still in the position found; support it above and below the injury.
2. Arm: a sling, or hold it against the body. Leg: pad around it; do not move the person unless in danger.
3. Cover any open wound with a dressing; control bleeding around, not on, a protruding bone.
4. Call the emergency number for leg, pelvis, spine or open fractures, or loss of feeling or colour beyond the injury.

Do not: straighten or push back; give food or drink (possible surgery); move someone with a suspected neck or back injury.

## 12. Seizure
1. Time it.
2. Clear the space around them; cushion the head.
3. Loosen anything tight at the neck. Do not restrain.
4. When the jerking stops, recovery position; stay until fully awake.

Call the emergency number if it lasts more than 5 minutes, repeats, is their first, they are injured, pregnant or diabetic, it happened in water, or they do not wake afterwards.

Do not: put anything in the mouth; hold them down; give food or drink until fully alert.

## 13. Stroke (FAST)
- **F**ace: ask for a smile; one side droops.
- **A**rms: raise both; one drifts down.
- **S**peech: slurred or wrong words.
- **T**ime: call the emergency number immediately and note when symptoms began.

Draw: front view, three small panels: an asymmetric mouth, one arm lower than the other, a speech mark, then a clock. Do not: give food, drink or aspirin; wait to see if it passes.

## 14. Heart attack
Chest pressure or pain that may spread to the arm, jaw or back; sweating, nausea, breathlessness. Signs can be milder in women, older people and people with diabetes.
1. Call the emergency number.
2. Sit them down, half-sitting with knees bent and back supported.
3. If not allergic and not told to avoid it, an adult can chew aspirin (**Varies:** 150–325 mg depending on the body) while waiting.
4. Help with their own prescribed nitroglycerin if they have it.
5. Be ready for CPR and an AED if they collapse.

## 15. Anaphylaxis and the adrenaline auto-injector
Signs: swelling of the lips, tongue or throat, difficulty breathing, wheeze, widespread hives, faintness, after exposure to an allergen.
1. Use the person's adrenaline (epinephrine) auto-injector at once. Remove the safety cap.
2. Hold it in a fist, press the tip firmly against the middle of the outer thigh (`thighNear`, outer side), through clothing if needed, until it clicks.
3. Hold in place (3–10 seconds depending on the device; read its label), then remove.
4. Call the emergency number and say "anaphylaxis".
5. Lie them down with legs raised; sitting up if breathing is hard. No standing or walking.
6. A second injector after 5 minutes if no improvement.

Do not: inject into the buttock, a vein, hands or feet; put a thumb over the end; let them stand up.

## 16. Fainting
Feeling faint: lie down and raise the legs, or sit with the head between the knees. Fainted: lay on the back, raise the legs about 30 cm, loosen tight clothing; they should recover within a minute or two. Not recovering quickly, or not breathing normally: treat as unresponsive (1).

## 17. Low blood sugar
Shaky, sweaty, confused, known diabetes. Awake and able to swallow: 15–20 g of fast sugar (glucose tablets, 150–200 mL of juice or regular soft drink), recheck after 15 minutes, repeat once, then a snack. Not able to swallow or unresponsive: nothing by mouth, recovery position, call the emergency number.

## 18. Minor wounds
1. Wash your hands.
2. Rinse the wound under clean running water for several minutes; wash around it with soap.
3. Press with a clean cloth until bleeding stops.
4. Cover with a sterile dressing or plaster; change daily and when wet.

See a professional for deep, gaping or dirty wounds, bites, wounds that will not stop bleeding, signs of infection (spreading redness, pus, fever), or an out-of-date tetanus vaccination.

## 19. Subcutaneous injection (insulin pen)
The dose is whatever was prescribed; the sheet shows technique only.
1. Wash hands. Check the insulin type, expiry and appearance.
2. Attach a new needle. Prime with 2 units, needle up, until a drop appears.
3. Dial the prescribed dose.
4. Choose a site: abdomen (keep about 5 cm, two to three finger widths, away from the navel; below the ribs, above the hip line, out to the flanks), outer thigh, back of the upper arm, buttock. Rotate: at least 1 cm (a finger width) from the last injection; a common scheme divides the abdomen into four quadrants, one per week, moving clockwise. Avoid scars, moles, bruises and lumps (lipohypertrophy: fatty lumps from repeated injections in one spot, which absorb insulin erratically).
5. Insert at 90° with a 4 mm needle. Longer needles, or very lean adults and children, may need a lifted skin fold or 45°.
6. Press the button fully and count to 10 (the pen's leaflet may say 6) before withdrawing straight out.
7. Do not rub. Needle into a sharps container.

Draw: a close-up cross-section of skin (see `anatomy.md`: skin about 2 mm, fat beneath, then muscle) with the needle ending in the fat is the panel that teaches the idea.

## 20. Subcutaneous injection (insulin syringe and vial)
The dose is whatever was prescribed; the sheet shows technique only. Use a syringe that matches the insulin strength (U-100 syringe for U-100 insulin).
1. Wash hands. Check the insulin type, expiry and appearance. Wipe the vial's rubber stopper with alcohol and let it dry.
2. Pull the plunger back to draw in air equal to the dose. With the vial upright, push the needle through the stopper and inject the air (this stops a vacuum forming).
3. Turn vial and syringe upside down together, needle tip under the liquid, and pull the plunger to the dose mark. Bubbles: flick the barrel, push them back into the vial, draw again to the mark.
4. Choose and rotate the site as for a pen (19).
5. Insert in one quick motion at 90°, the syringe making a "T" with the skin. **Varies:** with a 6 mm syringe needle most adults need no skin fold; children, lean adults and longer needles (8 mm and over) need a gently lifted fold of skin and fat (not muscle), or a 45° angle.
6. Push the plunger all the way in, steadily. Count to 10 before withdrawing (**Varies:** some teaching says 5 seconds for a syringe).
7. Withdraw at the same angle, then release any skin fold. Do not rub. Do not recap: the whole syringe goes straight into a sharps container.

Do not: reuse or share syringes; inject cold insulin straight from the fridge if it stings (let it reach room temperature); shake the vial hard (cloudy insulins are rolled gently); mix insulins unless taught to.

Draw: the syringe and vial are the subject for steps 2 and 3 (`m.syringe`, `m.vial`), with the plunger and the liquid moving; the skin section with a fold (`m.skinSection({ fold })`) for steps 5 and 6.

## 21. Hand washing
Wet, soap, then about 20 seconds covering: palms, backs of hands, between the fingers, backs of fingers in the opposite palm, thumbs, fingertips and nails, wrists. Rinse, dry with a clean towel, turn the tap off with the towel. Draw as hand close-ups, one per motion.
